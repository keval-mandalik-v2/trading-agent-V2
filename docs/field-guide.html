01

Reading a chart

Before any strategy makes sense, the picture has to. A price chart is not a line — it is a row of small boxes, each one summarising a fixed slice of time.

Candlecandlestick · bar · OHLC

One box summarising a fixed period of time with exactly four numbers: the Open, the High, the Low and the Close. Every rule in this strategy is built from those four numbers and nothing else.

Timeframeinterval · resolution

How much time one candle covers. We use 15 minutes, so the trading day is chopped into 25 candles. A candle labelled 10:30 covers 10:30 up to 10:45 — and it is not finished until 10:45.

Why this bitesYour friend called it "the 10:30 setup", but the 10:30 candle only finishes at 10:45. Acting at 10:30 means acting on a candle that has not happened yet. That single confusion is one of the most common ways beginners fool themselves.
Sessiontrading day

One day of trading. Indian equity markets run 09:15 to 15:30 IST, Monday to Friday, minus holidays. The first candle is 09:15; the last is 15:15.

Volumeturnover · quantity

How many shares changed hands during a candle. Big volume is usually read as conviction — lots of people acting. It is the one number in the chart that is not a price.

Watch outNSE occasionally sends one extra bar at the end of the day carrying the entire day's volume. We saw HDFCBANK report 46,184,640 where a normal bar was around 2,000,000. Our code detects and discards those, because leaving one in would silently poison every volume comparison.
Gapgap up · gap down

When a stock opens at a very different price from where it closed the day before, because news arrived while the market was shut. The price never traded in between — it jumped.

Gaps are the reason a stop-loss cannot protect you overnight. A stop only works while the market is open.

02

The pattern itself

Your friend's setup is really two classic ideas stacked together: an opening range breakout supplies the box and the direction, and an inside bar consolidation filters out boxes that never went quiet.

Opening Range BreakoutORB · first candle breakout

The family this strategy belongs to. The idea: the first part of the day sets a range while everyone digests overnight news, and whichever side price eventually escapes shows the day's real direction. Very widely traded in India.

Inside barinside candle

A candle whose entire range — wicks and all — fits within an earlier candle's range. It means the market went quiet: nobody pushed price beyond where it had already been.

The word is inside, not "insider". Insider trading is an unrelated crime. They sound alike and mean nothing alike.

Candle 1the opening candle

The 09:15–09:30 candle. Its high and low become the reference box that every other rule of the day measures against — including where your stop-loss goes.

Coilthe setup · consolidation · the box

Our name for the qualifying pattern: candles 2, 3, 4 and 5 all sitting fully inside Candle 1. The mental image is a spring compressing — pressure building for a release.

Note this is stricter than a textbook inside bar. Each candle must be inside Candle 1 specifically, not merely inside the one before it.

How oftenAbout 13% of stock-days. Across 50 Nifty stocks that is roughly 6 to 8 Coils on a typical morning.
Pokebreach · false break

When one of the four candles sticks even a wick outside Candle 1's range. A single Poke disqualifies the whole day for that stock — there is no such thing as a partial Coil.

Why so strictWe tested loosening it. Allowing bodies to count instead of wicks tripled the number of setups — and dropped the success rate below break-even. Strictness was doing real work.
Breakoutthe break

A candle that closes beyond Candle 1's high or low. A wick that pokes out and snaps back does not count — only where the candle finishes.

Signalalert · trigger

A Coil plus a confirmed breakout, together with the direction it implies. Close above the box, the signal is BUY; close below, it is SELL. This is the thing that reaches your phone.

How oftenAbout 8% of stock-days, which works out to roughly 4 alerts a day across the Nifty 50. Most days you will hear very little.
Entryfill · entry price

The price you actually get in at: the open of the candle after the breakout candle. Not the breakout candle's own close — you cannot know a candle has closed until it has, so that price was never available to you.

Targettake-profit · TP

Where you get out with a profit: 1% beyond entry. Reaching it is the only outcome counted as a win.

Stopstop-loss · SL

Where you get out with a loss — here, Candle 1's opposite extreme. Its low if you bought, its high if you sold.

The critical thing to understand: this distance is chosen by the market, not by you. Some mornings Candle 1 is narrow and the stop sits 0.4% away. Some mornings it is 9% away.

The one that nearly hurtTHERMAX had a Candle 1 that was 9.13% wide, putting the stop 9.75% below entry. At 5× leverage, one such trade removes half a ₹20,000 account. This is why we restricted the strategy to Nifty 50 — and why position sizing, further down, matters more than anything else on this page.
Fizzletimeout · scratch

Our word for the outcome nobody warns you about: price breaks out, drifts, and reaches neither target nor stop before the day ends. You exit at whatever the market offers.

The real storyFizzles are 65% of all signals — by far the most common result. Your friend's description of the strategy covered only what happens the other third of the time.
Whipsawreversal · fake-out

Price breaks one way, drags you in, then reverses and breaks the other way. Painful, and common enough to have its own name.

MeasuredHappened on 10% of signals. Taking the reversal as a second trade lost money, so we take one signal per stock per day and let the reversals go.
03

Money and risk

This is the section that decides whether a correct prediction actually makes you money. Most beginners study patterns and skip this. It is backwards — the arithmetic here is larger than the edge it is applied to.

Intradayday trading

Buying and selling within the same session, holding nothing overnight. The opposite is positional or delivery trading, where you keep the shares.

MIS vs CNCintraday vs delivery product

Two ways to place the same order. MIS (Margin Intraday Square-off) lends you extra buying power but the broker force-closes your position around 15:20. CNC means you pay in full and can hold as long as you like.

These cannot be combined. Carrying a trade overnight means giving up the leverage — ₹20,000 buys ₹20,000 of stock, not ₹1,00,000.

Leveragemargin · 5x

Trading with more money than you have. "5×" means ₹20,000 controls ₹1,00,000 of stock. It multiplies gains and losses identically — it is not a strategy, it is a magnifying glass.

What 5× really didSame 88 trades, only the leverage changed. At fixed 5× the account fell to ₹18,520 with a 34% drawdown. Risking 1% per trade instead, it ended at ₹20,641 with a 3.6% drawdown.
Square-offauto square-off

The broker automatically closing your intraday position near 15:20, whether you like the price or not. Every Fizzle exits here.

Bid–ask spreadthe spread

The gap between the best price someone will buy at and the best price someone will sell at. You always buy at the higher one and sell at the lower one, so crossing the spread costs you money instantly — before the trade has done anything at all.

The killer factThis strategy's raw edge is about 0.08% per trade. On Nifty 50 stocks the spread alone is roughly 0.02–0.05% each way. The edge is roughly the width of the doorway you must walk through to collect it.
Slippageexecution cost

Getting a worse price than you intended, because the market moved between deciding and executing. It grows when the stock is thinly traded or moving fast.

Round-trip costbrokerage · STT · GST · charges

Everything you pay to get in and out once: brokerage, Securities Transaction Tax, exchange fees, stamp duty, SEBI charges and 18% GST layered on some of them.

Measured, not guessedOn a ₹1,00,000 intraday position: ₹106, or 0.106%. Against a 1% target that is over a tenth of your entire winnings — every single trade, win or lose.
Risk : RewardR:R

How much you stand to lose against how much you stand to gain. Risking ₹2.40 to make ₹1.00 is a ratio of 2.4 : 1 — against you.

Position sizequantity · lot size

How much money you put into one trade. Almost every beginner treats this as an afterthought. It is the single most powerful control you have.

Risk-based sizingfixed fractional · 1% rule

Instead of always buying a fixed amount, you choose a fixed loss — say ₹200 — and work backwards to how many shares that allows. A wide stop means a small position; a tight stop means a large one.

Fixed sizing asks "how much can I buy?" Risk-based sizing asks "how much can I lose?"

What it does to THERMAXRisking 1% of ₹20,000 = ₹200 per trade, regardless of stop distance:
stop 0.50% → position ₹40,000 → loss ₹200
stop 1.00% → position ₹20,000 → loss ₹200
stop 9.75% → position  ₹2,051 → loss ₹200
The account-killing 9.75% stop now costs exactly what every other trade costs.
Drawdownmax DD

The worst peak-to-trough fall your account suffers along the way. Two strategies can end at the same place, with one of them having been terrifying to hold. Drawdown measures the terror.

04

Telling truth from luck

This section contains the ideas that separate people who make money from people who have stories. None of them are about charts.

Hit Ratewin rate · accuracy · strike rate

The share of trades that reach target. The number everyone quotes and almost nobody qualifies.

A hit rate without its target and stop attached is not information. It is like being told a car "does 40" — forty what?

Your friend's 85% is real — and uselessSet the target to 0.5% and this strategy hits 77% of decided trades. Set it to 1% and it hits 58%. The 77% version loses money twice as fast, because the wins are half the size. High win rates are the easiest thing in trading to manufacture.
Breakeven Hit Raterequired win rate

The hit rate at which you make exactly nothing, derived from your stop distance, your target and your costs. Any claimed win rate is meaningless until compared against this number.

Why it is on every alertRisking 2.43% to make 1% needs a 71% hit rate just to stand still. So an "80% accurate" strategy with that shape is barely profitable — and a 65% one bleeds slowly while you win most of your trades.
Expectancyedge · average return per trade

The average rupees a trade makes you, wins and losses blended. This — not win rate — is the number that determines whether you get richer.

You can win 6 trades out of 10 and go broke. You can win 3 out of 10 and get rich. Expectancy is why.

Sample sizen

How many observations a conclusion rests on. Small samples produce confident nonsense.

The number nobody says out loudTo prove an edge of +0.079% per trade is real rather than luck takes about 328 trades — 1.3 years of daily trading. Your friend showed you three charts.
Confidence intervalerror bars · 95% CI

The honest range your true answer probably lies in. If that range contains zero, you have not shown anything at all.

Our best-looking rule"Take the narrowest Candle 1 each day" returned +0.179% per trade — but its interval ran from −0.120% to +0.478%. Zero sits comfortably inside. That rule might be good, might be worthless; 23 trades cannot tell.
Permutation testrandomisation check

A way to ask "could a monkey have done this?" You simulate thousands of random choices and see how many beat your clever rule.

The humbling resultWe simulated 20,000 people picking one signal a day at random. 10.8% of them matched or beat our best rule. About one blindfolded coin-flipper in nine did as well. Would you buy their system?
Multiple comparisonsp-hacking · data mining

Test enough ideas against the same data and some will look brilliant purely by chance. Test seven theories about a cricket match and one will be right — that is not cricket knowledge.

Our own confessionWe tested 46 variants against the same 88 signals. At roughly a one-in-nine chance of any single one looking good by luck, four or five false winners were expected. Exactly four appeared.

The right first question about any backtest is never "what were the returns?" It is "how many things did you try?"
Overfittingcurve-fitting

Tuning rules until they fit the past beautifully and the future not at all. The tell-tale sign is a rule with lots of oddly specific numbers in it.

The defence: choose rules because they make sense first, and only then check the number. Never the other way round.

Survivorship biashighlight reel

Judging something by its successes because the failures were never shown to you. Nobody screenshots their losers.

The three chartsYou were shown DATAPATTNS, THERMAX and HDFCLIFE. Those same stocks also produced stop-outs in the same period that nobody photographed. It is like rating a restaurant from the pictures on its own wall.
Lookahead biasfuture leak

Accidentally using information that would not have been available at the time. The most common way a backtest reports profits that vanish in live trading.

It hides in innocent places: entering at a candle's closing price, or deciding which alert you "would have taken" after seeing how they turned out.

Base Rateprior · what usually happens

How things have historically turned out for cases like this one. It replaces opinion with evidence — and it earns its authority slowly.

Why your alerts say "insufficient data"The original design attached a confident 0–100 confidence score. We tested it: one factor was true by definition and handed every signal a free 20 points, and the factor with the clearest real signal was weighted lowest. We deleted it. The alert now says "insufficient data (n=0, need 20)" until it has genuinely earned the right to say otherwise.
05

Words we invented

These are not industry terms — they are specific to your system, and they exist because each one names something that would otherwise get muddled.

Journaldata/signals.csv

The permanent record of every signal the system ever produced and what happened to it. Stored as files committed to your GitHub repository, which means every entry carries a timestamp you cannot quietly edit later.

Pickyour TAKE or SKIP answer

Your decision on a signal, recorded before the outcome exists, stamped with Telegram's own clock. Answer late and it is logged as late.

This exists to answer a question almost no trader can answer about themselves: does my judgment beat picking at random? Decide after seeing the results and you are measuring your memory, not your skill.

Research logdocs/research-log.md

An append-only list of every idea ever tested against the Journal — including the failures. It exists so the multiple-comparisons count is always known.

Delete a failed test and every future claim silently inflates.

ADRarchitecture decision record

A short note recording a decision and, more importantly, why. Your project has seven. They stop a future version of you from "fixing" something that was deliberate — such as widening the stock universe and quietly reintroducing 9.75% stops.

Backtesthistorical simulation

Running the rules over past data to see what would have happened. Cheap, fast, and extremely easy to fool yourself with — which is what most of section 04 is about.

Paper tradingsimulated trading

Recording decisions without money at stake. Your system is a paper lab: it has no broker connection and cannot place an order. The worst thing it can do to you is send a Telegram message.

The honest verdict

The pattern is real. It appears on schedule, about 13% of stock-days, exactly as described. There is even a genuine edge in it — around 0.08% per trade.

That edge is smaller than the bid–ask spread you must cross to collect it. After costs the expected return is +0.006% per trade, with a confidence interval that comfortably contains zero. It is not a scam and it is not a goldmine. It is a coin flip that pays the broker.

The claimed 80–85% is achievable — at a 0.5% target — and that version loses money faster. And of 88 signals, not one ever moved 3% in its favour; the median best-case move was +0.51%. The +8% and +13% charts you were shown belong to midcaps, which this system deliberately does not trade.

Your friend is almost certainly not lying to you. He genuinely watched those stocks run. He has simply never counted his Fizzles, never priced his spread, and never asked himself how many variations he tried before finding the one he showed you.

That is not dishonesty. That is what trading looks like without a research log — which is the thing your system has and his does not.

You found all of this out for ₹0. The version of you that funded the account first would have found it out at roughly −14% a month.
